# Day 1 Lesson Plan — "What Is Coding?"
**Time:** 10:00 AM – 12:00 PM  
**Theme:** Coding is giving instructions clearly  
**Audience:** Ages 6–14, grouped in 5s (10 groups)  
**Volunteer prep:** Place 10–15 LEGO bricks per table before class. Mix of colours — at minimum include red, blue, yellow, green.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of Day 1, kids should be able to:
- Define coding in their own words
- Recognise that computers follow step-by-step instructions
- Build and follow a basic algorithm using LEGO stacking
- Understand that order matters — the wrong sequence gives the wrong result
- Complete an introductory coding activity on the Blockly Stack Builder

---

## 10:00–10:15 | Welcome & Icebreaker — "Tower of Names"

**Facilitator Script:**
> "Hi everyone! 👋🏽 [introduce yourself and the lesson]. Before we do anything else — look at the bricks on your table. Everyone pick ONE brick. We're going to go around and each person says their name and the colour of their brick."

**Activity — Tower of Names:**
- After introductions, each group stacks their 5 bricks one at a time as each person says their name — in the order they introduced themselves.
- The result is a group "name tower." Each colour layer represents a person.

**Why this works:** It's the first algorithm of the day — a sequence of actions (say name → place brick) repeated 5 times. You can name this at the end of the icebreaker.

> "Look at that! You just followed a sequence — the same order, every time. That's the first idea we're exploring today."

**Goal:** Get kids relaxed, hands on bricks immediately, and introduced to sequencing without calling it that yet.

---

## 10:15–10:30 | Mini Lesson: What Is Coding?

**Facilitator Prompt Questions:**
- "What do computers do?" *(Take answers — phones, games, tablets, etc.)*
- "Do they think for themselves?" *(Nope!)*
- "So how do they know what to do?"

**Analogy — Walk to Me:**
Ask a volunteer to walk to you. Give them step-by-step instructions mid-walk.
> "Computers are just like that. They only do what you tell them — and they do EXACTLY what you say, nothing more."

> "Humans write code — step-by-step instructions that tell the computer what to do. That's programming. And today you're going to practice writing instructions... with LEGO."

---

## 10:30–10:50 | Activity — Human Robot Game

**Reference video:** https://youtu.be/p7_wNgf2rZk?si=CZwK8taqNz64iXy3

**How it works:**
- One child is the "robot," one child is the "programmer"
- The programmer gives step-by-step verbal instructions. The robot follows them exactly — no guessing, no filling in gaps
- Use physical movement (walk here, turn left, pick up the brick, place it on the table)

**LEGO connection:**  
At the end of the movement game, extend it to bricks:
> "Now the robot's job is to pick up bricks. Programmer — tell your robot exactly which colour brick to pick up and where to place it. Robot — do ONLY what you're told."

**Facilitator tip:** Pause halfway and ask: *"What happened when the instructions weren't clear?"*  
Let them reflect on the importance of sequencing and accuracy.

---

## 10:50–11:20 | LEGO Challenge — "Stack It Like I Say"

This is the core unplugged activity. It directly models how a computer executes code.

**Setup:** Each group stays with their 10–15 bricks. Partners sit back-to-back or one person covers their build.

### How it works:

**Round 1 — Describe a Stack**
1. Partner A secretly builds a stack of 4–5 bricks (specific colours in a specific order, bottom to top).
2. Partner A describes their stack step-by-step:
   > "Put the red brick down first. Then place the blue brick on top of the red one. Then yellow on top of the blue."
3. Partner B builds what they hear — using only the instructions, no peeking.
4. Compare the two stacks. Did they match?
5. Switch roles.

**Facilitator Reflection Questions:**
- "Was your partner's stack exactly like yours?"
- "Which instruction was the hardest to follow? Why?"
- "What would you change about how you described it?"

**Why stacking works better than flat building:**  
With a flat build, small errors are hidden. With a stack, a wrong colour or wrong position is immediately visible from the side. The match or mismatch is obvious — exactly like a computer showing you the wrong output because your code was slightly off.

### Round 2 — Debug It (if time allows)
Partner A builds a 4-brick stack. Partner B tries to describe it *back* to Partner A — and A corrects any mistakes.  
Introduce the word **debug**: *"In coding, when something goes wrong, we fix it. That's called debugging."*

## 11:20–11:45 | Blockly Activity — Stack Builder

**Launch Talking Point:**
> "You've been giving instructions to your partner. Now let's give instructions to a computer! We're going to use a tool called Blockly — you drag blocks of instructions and the computer follows them exactly. Sound familiar?"

**File:** `day1-stack-builder.html` — open in any browser, no internet needed.

**What kids see:**
- A workspace where they drag brick blocks (🧱 place RED brick, 🧱 place BLUE brick, etc.)
- A "Target Stack" — the exact stack they need to match
- A "Your Stack" — what their code actually produces when they click Run
- 4 levels of increasing challenge

**Level breakdown:**

| Level | Task | Concept reinforced |
|-------|------|--------------------|
| 1 | Stack red → blue → yellow | Basic sequencing |
| 2 | Stack 4 bricks in order | Longer sequences, more precision |
| 3 | Stack red → blue → red → blue | Recognising patterns (preview of loops) |
| 4 | Free design: build any 5-brick stack, describe it to a partner | Creative application + peer communication |

**Support tips:**
- Help younger kids (6–8) read the colour names on the blocks
- For kids who finish fast: challenge them to describe their Level 4 stack to a partner who then tries to recreate it — back to the physical activity
- Volunteers should circulate and ask: *"Why did your stack come out wrong? Which block was in the wrong place?"*

**Key question to ask every group:**
> "Is what the computer built the same as what you meant? Why or why not?"

## 11:45–12:00 | Reflection & Badge Time

**Facilitator Wrap-Up Questions:**
- "Who can tell me one thing they learned today?"
- "Did anyone help a friend figure out their stack? You get teamwork points!"
- "What happens if you put the instructions in the wrong order?"

**Badge of the Day:** 🎖 **"Instruction Explorer"**  
Earned by any child who:
- Participated in the Human Robot Game
- Completed at least Level 1 of the Blockly Stack Builder
- Can say what coding is in their own words

**Closing line:**
> "Today you were the programmers. Tomorrow we'll learn a shortcut that makes writing long instructions way faster. See you then!"

---

## Volunteer Notes

- **Before class:** Place 10–15 mixed-colour bricks on each table. Try to ensure every group has at least: 2 red, 2 blue, 2 yellow, 2 green bricks.
- **During LEGO activity:** Don't correct kids' stacks for them — let the mismatch happen. The wrong output is the lesson.
- **During Blockly:** If a device isn't working, pair two kids on one screen. The verbal discussion that happens is actually valuable.
- **Timing:** If the Human Robot Game runs long, compress Round 2 of the LEGO challenge. Don't skip the Blockly activity — it's the bridge from physical to digital.
