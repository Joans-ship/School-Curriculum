# Day 2 — Loops: "Repeat Smarter, Not Harder"
**Theme:** When something needs to be done again and again — use a loop!
 
## 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of Day 2, kids should be able to:
- Explain what a loop is in their own words
- Identify the repeating unit inside a pattern
- Build a repeating stack using LEGO bricks
- Use a repeat block in Blockly to produce the same stack with fewer instructions
---
 
## 10:00–10:15 | Recap Game — "What's the Code?"
 
Pass a soft block or LEGO brick around the circle. Whoever holds it completes one sentence:
- "Coding is…"
- "An algorithm is…"
- "Yesterday I built…"
Celebrate everyone who earned the **Instruction Explorer** badge yesterday — quick shoutouts by name.
 
**Facilitator tip:** Keep this moving. Every child should get one turn but no more than 15 seconds each.

## 10:15–10:35 | Mini Lesson + 🎬 Coding Clubhouse Video
 
**Introduce sequences through movement:**
> "Watch me. Clap → hop → spin → sit. Was that a sequence? YES! What would happen if I did it out of order?"
 
Walk through the idea that a sequence is doing things in the right order — like getting dressed, or stacking bricks bottom-to-top.
 
**Transition to loops:**
> "Now what if I asked you to clap → hop → spin → sit… ten times? Would you write it out ten times? No! You'd say: repeat 4 times. That shortcut is called a loop."

## 10:35–11:00 | LEGO Challenge — "Loop Stack"
 
This is the core unplugged activity. It makes loops *physical and visible.*
 
**Setup:** 10–15 bricks per group. Each group needs at least 2 colours repeated (e.g. 4 red, 4 blue, or 3 red, 3 yellow, 3 green).
 
### Round 1 — Build the Loop
Each group receives a **Loop Challenge Card** (a printed card showing a colour pattern, e.g. red-blue-red-blue-red-blue).
 
Steps:
1. Kids look at the card and identify the **repeating unit** — the section that keeps coming back.
   > "What is the smallest chunk that repeats? Red-blue. How many times? Three times."
2. They write or say the loop aloud before building:
   > "Repeat 3 times: place red, place blue."
3. Then they stack the bricks to match the card — bottom to top.
**Why stacking matters here:** When you build a repeating pattern upward, the rhythm is *physical* — your hands naturally feel the repeat. A flat build hides this. The tower shows it.
 
### Round 2 — Count the Difference
After building, ask:
- "How many bricks did you place?" *(e.g. 6)*
- "How many instructions did your loop need?" *(e.g. 3: repeat 3 times, place red, place blue)*
- "Which is faster — 6 instructions or 3?"
This is the entire point of loops. Let kids feel the difference themselves.
 
### Round 3 — Design Your Own (if time allows)
Each group invents a loop pattern using their bricks, writes it as: **"Repeat ___ times: [colour], [colour]"**, then challenges another group to build it from the description.
 
**Facilitator phrases to encourage:**
- "Repeat red-yellow two times."
- "We used a 3-colour loop: green-yellow-blue."

## 11:00–11:25 | Blockly Activity — Loop Builder
 
**What the activity should look like:**
 
Kids open the Loop Builder file on their device. The workspace has two categories of blocks: a **Repeat** block and individual **Place [colour] brick** blocks.
 
The screen shows a **Target Stack** (the pattern to match) and a **Your Output** column that fills in when they click Run.
 
There are 4 levels:
- **Level 1:** Reproduce a 6-brick red-blue-red-blue-red-blue pattern. The trick: you can do it with 6 individual blocks, but the challenge is to use a repeat block to do it in 4. Both approaches work — but the efficient version is highlighted.
- **Level 2:** An 8-brick green-yellow pattern repeated 4 times.
- **Level 3:** A 9-brick 3-colour pattern (red-blue-yellow × 3). Requires a repeat wrapping 3 brick blocks.
- **Level 4:** Free design — kids pick any 2 colours and a repeat count, build their own pattern, then describe it to their group in loop language: *"My loop repeats red and blue 4 times."*
The key feature: a **block counter** that shows how many blocks they used. When kids solve the pattern with fewer blocks than the naive solution, the counter turns green. This makes the efficiency of loops tangible.
 
**Facilitator prompt for every group:**
> "Did your loop produce the same stack as placing each brick one by one? So why would a programmer choose the loop?"
